## Position stand

<a id="garber-2011-acsm-position-stand-exercise-quantity"></a>
### Quantity and Quality of Exercise for Developing and Maintaining Cardiorespiratory, Musculoskeletal, and Neuromotor Fitness in Apparently Healthy Adults

Garber CE, Blissmer B, Deschenes MR, Franklin BA, Lamonte MJ, Lee IM, Nieman DC, Swain DP (2011). *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 43(7):1334-1359*.

`garber-2011-acsm-position-stand-exercise-quantity` · Position stand · Open licence (CC BY (repository or manuscript copy only)) · Copy: Citation only

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e318213fefb) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)

**Summary (initial read):** American College of Sports Medicine position stand (superseding the 1998 stand) on exercise prescription for apparently healthy adults of all ages. It recommends moderate-intensity cardiorespiratory exercise for at least 30 min on at least 5 days/week, or vigorous exercise for at least 20 min on at least 3 days/week, or an equivalent combination (about 500-1000 MET-min/week), plus resistance training on 2-3 days/week, neuromotor exercise, flexibility work on at least 2 days/week, and reduced sedentary time.

**Why it is here:** Authoritative baseline for minimum weekly session frequency (e.g., >=3 vigorous days/week) and for adding 2-3 strength days, which the coach can use as default frequency targets.

**Also tagged:** Coaching and training principles, Strength and cross-training, Beginners and adherence, Organization guides and plan templates, Increasing distance (volume progression)

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

The purpose of this Position Stand is to provide guidance to professionals who counsel and prescribe individualized exercise to apparently healthy adults of all ages. These recommendations also may apply to adults with certain chronic diseases or disabilities, when appropriately evaluated and advised by a health professional. This document supersedes the 1998 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) Position Stand, "The Recommended Quantity and Quality of Exercise for Developing and Maintaining Cardiorespiratory and Muscular Fitness, and Flexibility in Healthy Adults." The scientific evidence demonstrating the beneficial effects of exercise is indisputable, and the benefits of exercise far outweigh the risks in most adults. A program of regular exercise that includes cardiorespiratory, resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor exercise training beyond activities of daily living to improve and maintain physical fitness and health is essential for most adults. The ACSM recommends that most adults engage in moderate-intensity cardiorespiratory exercise training for ≥30 min·d on ≥5 d·wk for a total of ≥150 min·wk, vigorous-intensity cardiorespiratory exercise training for ≥20 min·d on ≥3 d·wk (≥75 min·wk), or a combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity exercise to achieve a total energy expenditure of ≥500-1000 MET·min·wk. On 2-3 d·wk, adults should also perform resistance exercises for each of the major muscle groups, and neuromotor exercise involving balance, agility, and coordination. Crucial to maintaining joint range of movement, completing a series of flexibility exercises for each the major muscle-tendon groups (a total of 60 s per exercise) on ≥2 d·wk is recommended. The exercise program should be modified according to an individual's habitual physical activity, physical function, health status, exercise responses, and stated goals. Adults who are unable or unwilling to meet the exercise targets outlined here still can benefit from engaging in amounts of exercise less than recommended. In addition to exercising regularly, there are health benefits in concurrently reducing total time engaged in sedentary pursuits and also by interspersing frequent, short bouts of standing and physical activity between periods of sedentary activity, even in physically active adults. Behaviorally based exercise interventions, the use of behavior change strategies, supervision by an experienced fitness instructor, and exercise that is pleasant and enjoyable can improve adoption and adherence to prescribed exercise programs. Educating adults about and screening for signs and symptoms of CHD and gradual progression of exercise intensity and volume may reduce the risks of exercise. Consultations with a medical professional and diagnostic exercise testing for CHD are useful when clinically indicated but are not recommended for universal screening to enhance the safety of exercise.

## Meta-analysis

### Is running associated with a lower risk of all-cause, cardiovascular and cancer mortality, and is the more the better? A systematic review and meta-analysis

Pedisic Z, Shrestha N, Kovalchik S, Stamatakis E, Liangruenrom N, Grgic J, Titze S, Biddle SJH, Bauman AE, Oja P (2020). *British Journal of Sports Medicine 54(15):898-905*.

`pedisic-2020-running-mortality-dose-response` · Meta-analysis · Open licence (CC BY-NC (repository or manuscript copy only)) · Copy: Local (openly licensed) `library-local/03-training-frequency/pedisic-2020-running-mortality-dose-response.pdf`

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-100493) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31685526/)

**Summary (initial read):** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies from six prospective cohorts (232,149 adults, 25,951 deaths over 5.5-35 years). Any running was associated with 27% lower all-cause (HR 0.73), 30% lower cardiovascular (HR 0.70) and 23% lower cancer mortality (HR 0.77) compared with no running. Meta-regression found no significant dose-response trends for weekly frequency, duration, pace or total volume, so even running once a week appears better than none, while higher doses may not add further mortality benefit.

**Why it is here:** Supports telling health-focused users that even 1-2 runs per week carry most of the longevity benefit, so a low-frequency plan is legitimate.

**Also tagged:** Beginners and adherence

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

Objective To investigate the association of running participation and the dose of running with the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular and cancer mortality. Design Systematic review and meta-analysis. Data sources Journal articles, conference papers and doctoral theses indexed in Academic Search Ultimate, CINAHL, Health Source: Nursing/Academic Edition, MasterFILE Complete, Networked Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations, Open Access Theses and Dissertations, PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, SPORTDiscus and Web of Science. Eligibility criteria for selecting studies Prospective cohort studies on the association between running or jogging participation and the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular and/or cancer mortality in a non-clinical population of adults were included. Results Fourteen studies from six prospective cohorts with a pooled sample of 232 149 participants were included. In total, 25 951 deaths were recorded during 5.5-35 year follow-ups. Our meta-analysis showed that running participation is associated with 27%, 30% and 23% lower risk of all-cause (pooled adjusted hazard ratio (HR)=0.73; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.68 to 0.79), cardiovascular (HR=0.70; 95% CI 0.49 to 0.98) and cancer (HR=0.77; 95% CI 0.68 to 0.87) mortality, respectively, compared with no running. A meta-regression analysis showed no significant dose-response trends for weekly frequency, weekly duration, pace and the total volume of running. Conclusion Increased rates of participation in running, regardless of its dose, would probably lead to substantial improvements in population health and longevity. Any amount of running, even just once a week, is better than no running, but higher doses of running may not necessarily be associated with greater mortality benefits.

**Summary (initial read):** Meta-analyses of RCTs of endurance running in healthy, physically inactive adults aged 18-65 (35 of 49 included articles pooled). Typical programs used about 3.7 sessions and 2.3 h of running per week at 60-90% of maximal heart rate for about 21.5 weeks. After about a year, running reduced body mass (-3.3 kg), body fat (-2.7%), resting heart rate (-6.7 bpm) and triglycerides (-16.9 mg/dL) and increased VO2max (+7.1 ml/kg/min) and HDL cholesterol (+3.3 mg/dL); only 10 of 161 outcomes could be pooled.

**Why it is here:** Describes the weekly frequency/duration (about 3-4 runs, ~2.3 h/week) used in successful running programs for inactive adults, a template for the coach's beginner frequency.

## Systematic review

<a id="johnston-2018-training-load-injury-endurance-review"></a>
### The associations between training load and baseline characteristics on musculoskeletal injury and pain in endurance sport populations: A systematic review

Johnston R, Cahalan R, O'Keeffe M, O'Sullivan K, Comyns T (2018). *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport 21(9):910-918*.

`johnston-2018-training-load-injury-endurance-review` · Systematic review · Open licence (CC BY-NC-SA (repository or manuscript copy only)) · Copy: Citation only

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.03.001) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29559317/)

**Summary (initial read):** Prospectively registered systematic review of 12 studies in endurance athletes (runners, triathletes, rowers). Higher weekly or monthly training distance, fewer than two sessions per week, and very low weekly or high monthly training duration were associated with more injury or pain (medium-to-large effects); baseline risk factors included previous injury, age over 45, non-musculoskeletal comorbidities and older running shoes. No included study measured internal load or acute:chronic workload ratios.

**Why it is here:** Supports a minimum of two runs per week and caution with high weekly distance, and notes the absence of ACWR evidence in endurance populations.

**Also tagged:** Increasing distance (volume progression), Injury risk and training errors

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

Objectives To determine the associations between training load, baseline characteristics (e.g. age or previous injury) and rate of musculoskeletal injury and/or pain specifically within an Endurance Sporting Population (ESP). Design Prospectively registered systematic review. Methods Eight electronic databases were searched by two independent reviewers. Studies were required to prospectively monitor both (i) training loads and (ii) musculoskeletal injury and/or pain for >3 months. Methodological quality and risk of bias were determined utilising the Critical Skills Appraisal Program (CASP). Reported effect sizes were categorised as small, medium or large. Results Twelve endurance sport studies were eligible (running, triathlon, rowing). Increased injury and/or pain risk was associated with: (i) high total training distances per week/month (medium effect size) (ii) training frequency <2 sessions/week (medium effect size) and (iii) both low weekly (<2hours/week) and high monthly (large effect size) training durations. None of the studies reported internal training load data or acute:chronic workload ratios. Baseline characteristics found to increase the rate of injury and/or pain included: (i) a history of previous injury (medium effect size), (ii) age >45 years (small effect size), (iii) non-musculoskeletal comorbidities (large effect size), (iv) using older running shoes (small effect size) and (v) non-competitive behaviour. Conclusions This review identifies a range of external training load factors and baseline characteristics associated with an increased rate of injury and/or pain within ESPs. There is an absence of research relating to internal training loads and acute:chronic workload ratios in relation to rate of injury and/or pain within ESPs.

<a id="pollock-1977-frequency-duration-attrition-injury"></a>
### Effects of frequency and duration of training on attrition and incidence of injury

Pollock ML, Gettman LR, Milesis CA, Bah MD, Durstine JL, Johnson RB (1977). *Medicine and Science in Sports 9(1):31-36*.

`pollock-1977-frequency-duration-attrition-injury` · Randomized controlled trial · Paywalled · Copy: Citation only

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-197621000-00097) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/870782/) · [Web](https://scholarcommons.sc.edu/sph_physical_activity_public_health_facpub/262)

**Summary (initial read):** Classic randomized training study in previously sedentary men (prison/jail inmates) assigned to control or to jogging programs that differed in session duration and weekly frequency, run at 85-90% of maximal heart rate. Cardiorespiratory fitness improved with greater frequency and duration, but injury rates rose too, from 0% in the lowest-frequency group to 54% in the longest-duration group; the authors advised against such high-frequency/long-duration programs for beginning joggers.

**Why it is here:** Foundational evidence that beginners running 5 days/week or long sessions get injured far more often than at 3 days/week, supporting a conservative starting frequency in the coach.

**Also tagged:** Beginners and adherence, Injury risk and training errors

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

Eighty-seven male inmates from a state prison and 70 inmates from a county jail volunteered as subjects. The subjects, age 20 to 35 yrs, were assigned randomly into a control or exercise group. Their Vo2max and treadmill performance values were determined before and after a 20 week jogging program. Training intensity was between 85 and 90 percent of maximum heart rate and involved workouts 3 days/week for 15, 30, or 45-min duration at the state prison and for 30-min 1, 3, or 5 days/week at the country jail. <a id="toresdahl-2022-injuries-first-time-marathon-runners"></a>
### Factors associated with injuries in first-time marathon runners from the New York City marathon

Toresdahl BG, McElheny K, Metzl JD, Kinderknecht JJ, Quijano B, et al. (2022). *The Physician and Sportsmedicine 50(3):227-232*.

`toresdahl-2022-injuries-first-time-marathon-runners` · Cohort study · Paywalled · Copy: Citation only

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913847.2021.1907257) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33750264/)

**Summary (initial read):** Prospective study of first-time marathoners registered for the 2017 New York City Marathon, followed from 12 weeks before the race (675 of 720 completed). 9.5% had a major injury that prevented starting or finishing and 49.2% a minor injury. Having completed a half marathon beforehand lowered the odds of injury (OR 0.40), averaging four or more runs per week was associated with more training injuries, and a longer longest training run was associated with fewer race-day injuries (OR 0.87 per unit distance).

**Why it is here:** Directly informs first-marathon plans: suggest a half marathon first, build an adequate longest run, and be cautious about scheduling four or more runs per week for first-timers.

**Also tagged:** Preparing for an event, Beginners and adherence, Increasing distance (volume progression)

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

Objectives To determine how baseline characteristics of first-time marathon runners and training patterns are associated with risk of injuries during training and the race. Methods First-time adult marathon runners who were registered for the 2017 New York City Marathon were monitored starting 12 weeks prior to the race. Baseline data collection included demographics and running experience. Running frequency, distance, and injury occurrence were self-reported using online surveys every 2 weeks. Results A total of 720 runners participated of which 675 completed the study. There were 64/675 (9.5%) who had major injuries during training or the race that preventing starting or finishing the race. An additional 332 (49.2%) had minor injuries interfering with training and/or affecting race performance. Injury incidence was not significantly different based on age or sex. Runners who completed a half marathon prior to the study were less likely to report getting injured [multivariable odds ratio (OR) 0.40, (0.22, 0.76), p = 0.005]. Runners who averaged <4 training runs per week during the study were less likely to report getting injured compared to those who averaged ≥4 per week [relative risk 1.36, (1.13-1.63), p = 0.001]. Longest training run distance during the study was inversely associated with race-day injury incidence [OR 0.87 (0.81, 0.94), p < 0.001]. Conclusion Injuries are common among first-time marathon runners. We found that risk of injury during training was associated with lack of half marathon experience and averaging ≥4 training runs per week. Longer training runs were associated with a lower incidence of race-day injuries. These results can inform the development of targeted injury-prevention interventions.

### The NLstart2run study: Training-related factors associated with running-related injuries in novice runners

Kluitenberg B, van der Worp H, Huisstede BMA, Hartgens F, Diercks R, Verhagen E, van Middelkoop M (2016). *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport 19(8):642-646*.

**Summary (initial read):** Prospective cohort of 1696 novices in a 6-week Start to Run programme who logged every run and injury, analysed with time-varying training factors for the 7 days before each session. <a id="schnohr-2015-dose-of-jogging-mortality-copenhagen"></a>
### Dose of jogging and long-term mortality: the Copenhagen City Heart Study

Schnohr P, O'Keefe JH, Marott JL, Lange P, Jensen GB (2015). *Journal of the American College of Cardiology 65(5):411-419*.

`schnohr-2015-dose-of-jogging-mortality-copenhagen` · Cohort study · Free to read · Copy: Citation only

[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2014.11.023) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25660917/)

**Summary (initial read):** Copenhagen City Heart Study cohort of 1,098 healthy joggers and 3,950 healthy sedentary non-joggers followed from 2001. Jogging 1-2.4 h/week had the lowest mortality (HR 0.29, 95% CI 0.11-0.80), with the best results for jogging 2-3 times/week (or once a week or less) at a slow or average pace. Light joggers had the lowest risk (HR 0.22), while moderate (HR 0.66) and strenuous joggers (HR 1.97, 95% CI 0.48-8.14) did not differ significantly from sedentary non-joggers; the authors describe a U-shaped dose-response, although the very wide confidence interval for strenuous joggers shows that estimate is highly imprecise.

**Why it is here:** Supports 2-3 easy runs per week as a sufficient health dose and is frequently cited (and contested) in debates about whether 'more' running is harmful.

**Also tagged:** Beginners and adherence

<details><summary>Abstract (from Europe PMC)</summary>

Background People who are physically active have at least a 30% lower risk of death during follow-up compared with those who are inactive.
